Nature · Ocean

The Ocean's Hidden World

The deep ocean is one of Earth's least explored environments. What could still be waiting to be discovered?

Sunlight fades rapidly as you descend into the ocean. In the deep sea, pressure is enormous, temperatures are often low, and many animals have adapted to life in darkness.

How do deep-sea animals find food?

Some eat falling organic material called marine snow. Others hunt, filter food from the water, or live near special environments such as hydrothermal vents.

Scientists use remotely operated vehicles, submarines and underwater cameras to explore these places. New observations continue to improve our understanding of ocean life.

Space · Planets

The Planet Where a Day Lasts Longer Than a Year

Venus rotates so slowly that one rotation takes longer than one trip around the Sun.

Venus takes about 243 Earth days to rotate once relative to distant stars. It completes one orbit around the Sun in about 225 Earth days.

Why is Venus so hot?

Its thick atmosphere contains mostly carbon dioxide, which traps heat through an intense greenhouse effect. The surface is hot enough to melt lead.

Venus also rotates in the opposite direction to most planets. On Venus, the Sun would appear to rise in the west and set in the east.

Nature · Animals

The Animal With Three Hearts

Octopuses have three hearts and a remarkable nervous system spread throughout their bodies.

Two of an octopus's hearts pump blood through the gills, where oxygen enters the blood. The third pumps blood to the rest of the body.

What makes an octopus special?

An octopus has eight arms equipped with suckers. Its nervous system allows its arms to perform many movements while receiving information from the environment.

Octopuses can solve certain puzzles, open containers and change their skin colour and texture to blend into their surroundings or communicate.

History · Ancient world

The Mystery of the Egyptian Pyramids

How did ancient Egyptians organise the enormous effort required to build their pyramids?

The Great Pyramid of Giza was built during the reign of Pharaoh Khufu, more than 4,500 years ago. It was originally about 146.6 metres tall.

How were the pyramids built?

Archaeological evidence points to skilled workers, organised teams, quarries, transport routes and carefully planned construction. Workers used tools, sledges and other methods to move heavy materials.

Researchers continue to investigate the exact techniques used for different stages of construction. The pyramids are impressive examples of ancient Egyptian engineering.

Science · Weather

The Science Behind Lightning

A flash of lightning is a powerful electrical discharge created during a storm.

Inside storm clouds, collisions between ice particles and other water droplets help separate electrical charges. When the electric field becomes strong enough, a discharge can occur.

Why do we hear thunder?

Lightning heats the surrounding air extremely quickly. The air expands rapidly, producing a pressure wave that we hear as thunder.

Light reaches us much faster than sound. Counting the seconds between a flash and its thunder can help estimate how far away the lightning is, but always seek shelter inside a substantial building or enclosed vehicle during a storm.

Space · Saturn

The Planet With Spectacular Rings

Saturn's rings stretch across an enormous distance, but they are made of countless individual particles.

Saturn's rings contain pieces of water ice, rocky material and dust. The particles range from tiny grains to much larger chunks.

Are the rings solid?

No. From far away they look like solid bands, but they are made of many separate particles orbiting Saturn.

The rings are very wide but extremely thin compared with their width. Scientists study them to learn more about gravity, planetary formation and the history of Saturn's moons.

Nature · Insects

How Bees Find Their Way Home

Honeybees use several clues to navigate between flowers and their hive.

Bees can use the Sun's position, visual landmarks and patterns in the landscape to help them navigate. They also have an internal sense of direction.

How do bees share information?

A honeybee that finds a good source of food can perform a waggle dance. The direction and duration of parts of this dance communicate information about the food's direction and distance.

This behaviour helps other bees locate flowers. It is one of the best-known examples of communication in insects.

Science · Atmosphere

The Northern Lights Explained

Colourful lights can dance across the sky when charged particles interact with Earth's atmosphere.

The aurora borealis, or northern lights, occurs when energetic charged particles interact with gases high in Earth's atmosphere. The southern counterpart is called the aurora australis.

Where do the colours come from?

Different gases and altitudes produce different colours. Oxygen commonly produces green and sometimes red light, while nitrogen can contribute blue and purple colours.

Auroras are most commonly visible at high latitudes, although strong solar activity can make them visible farther from the poles.

Science · Microscopic life

The Tiny Animal That Can Survive Extreme Conditions

Tardigrades are tiny animals famous for surviving conditions that many other organisms cannot tolerate.

Tardigrades, sometimes called water bears, are microscopic animals found in environments ranging from moss to marine habitats.

How can they survive?

Some species can enter a dormant state called cryptobiosis when conditions become harsh. In this state, their metabolism drops dramatically, allowing them to endure certain periods of drying and other environmental stresses.

They are not indestructible. Survival depends on the species, the conditions and how long the animal is exposed to them.

Space · The universe

How Big Is the Universe?

The observable universe contains an extraordinary number of galaxies, stars and planets.

The observable universe is the region of the universe from which light has had time to reach us since the universe began expanding about 13.8 billion years ago.

How large is it?

Scientists estimate that the observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across. This is larger than 27.6 billion light-years because space has expanded while the light was travelling.

The entire universe may extend far beyond what we can observe. Scientists do not yet know whether it is finite or infinite.
